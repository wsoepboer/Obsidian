# Schools of Management Thought

The historical lineage of organisation & management theory, each responding to the limits of the one before it:

- **Frederick Taylor — Scientific Management (c. 1900).** Replace ad-hoc supervision with systematic analysis: time-and-motion study to standardise tasks, strict division of labour, and training — the first attempt to make management itself a discipline rather than instinct.
- **Henri Fayol — General Management Theory (c. 1900).** Zoomed out from the factory floor to the whole organisation: defined management as a set of functions (planning, organising, commanding, coordinating, controlling) applicable at any level.
- **Max Weber — Bureaucracy Theory (c. 1920).** Formal hierarchy, written rules, and impersonal, position-based authority as the way to make large organisations predictable and fair — the model most large institutions still run on.
- **Elton Mayo — Human Relations Movement (c. 1945).** The Hawthorne studies showed output rises when workers feel *noticed and valued*, not just when conditions improve — the first evidence that social/psychological factors drive productivity, pushing back against Taylor's purely mechanical view.
- **Rensis Likert — Neo-Human Relations (c. 1950).** Extended human relations into a systems view of management style, contrasting exploitative-authoritative through to participative styles and linking style to performance.
- **Kenneth Boulding — Systems Approach (c. 1950).** Organisations as open systems exchanging inputs/outputs with their environment — the conceptual root of treating environment as something organisations must continuously adapt to (see [[External Parties (Stakeholders)]]).
- **Lawrence & Lorsch — Contingency Approach (c. 1965).** There is no one best way to organise — the right structure depends on the organisation's specific environment and task (differentiation vs integration).
- **Recent organisational theories (1980+).** Increasing emphasis on flexibility, culture, and rapid environmental change — the lead-in to modern strategic management ([[Strategy & Strategic Management]]) and [[Organisational Culture]].

**Pattern to remember:** each school reacts to a blind spot in the last — Taylor (mechanical efficiency) → Fayol (whole-organisation functions) → Weber (formal structure) → Mayo (people/psychology) → Likert (leadership style) → Boulding (open systems) → Lawrence & Lorsch (it depends).
