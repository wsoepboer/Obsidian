## 1. Etymology of "technology"
Greek **technē** (τέχνη) = art, craft, skill, plus **-logia** (from *logos*) = word, reasoned discourse, systematic treatment. Literal meaning: *systematic discourse about craft*.

In Greek, *technologia* meant something narrower — systematic treatment of grammar and rhetoric. It entered English in the 17th century still meaning "a treatise on the arts". The modern sense (applied science, industrial practice) is 19th century. MIT, founded 1861, marks that shift.

> [!warning] Correction
> His gloss — "applying knowledge in your mind to the real world" — is a workable definition but is **not** the etymology. *Technē* is the doing; *logos* is the talking-about. The word points at knowledge **about** craft, not the application of it. Don't repeat his version as etymology in written work.

> [!note] What the deck actually says
> The slide cites Aristotle, Schatzberg (2006) and Salomon (1984), and rests on the German distinction between **Technik** (the technical activity) and **Technologie** (the academic principle), which English does not have. Salomon: after the Industrial Revolution the word came to mean *all technical activity based on the application of science to practical ends*. That is closer to Boris's spoken gloss than to the strict etymology, so both readings are defensible if you name the source.

## 3. First technological applications
Student guesses: wheel, rocks/tools, fire, weapons. **His answer: pressure flaking** — shaped stone tools.

1. **Pressure flaking** — shaped edges for butchering, hide-scraping, hunting.
2. → **Hide-working → clothing** → migration into colder climates.
3. **Fire → cooking.** The cooked-food hypothesis: cooked food digests more easily, yielding more calories per unit of chewing and gut work, freeing metabolic budget for a larger brain. The deck's own speaker note concedes the evidence is **inconclusive**.
4. **Fire → community.** The hearth as gathering point.
5. **Language as technology.** The *table* example: the word is an arbitrary agreed convention, not genetic.
6. **Agriculture** → settlement, predictable yields, "the beginning of civilization".
7. **Clothing**, then **sailing**. The deck's sequence continues: **pottery → the wheel → electricity → the telegraph → neural interfaces**.

> [!warning] Contestable
> Language-as-technology is defensible under his definition, but language may be closer to an evolved capacity than an invented artifact. The specific *words* are conventions; the underlying faculty probably isn't.

> [!warning] Soft dates
> His sequencing is loose — he says so himself. The deck's speaker notes give **~2 Mya** for the first stone tools and **~75 kya** for pressure flaking.
> - Earliest stone tools ~3.3 Mya; Oldowan ~2.6 Mya
> - Clothing ~100-170 kya (body-lice genetic evidence) — far later than tools
> - Control of fire is genuinely disputed: ~1.5 Mya at the earliest claim, solid evidence only ~300-400 kya
>
> Treat any specific date he asserts as soft.

## 6. Economic growth & GDP
His claim: technological innovation → long-term economic growth. The deck asserts the same in one line.

**GDP** = total market value of final goods and services produced within a country in a period. Three measurement approaches: **output**, **expenditure**, **income**. His "value added in salaries" is the income approach.

> [!warning] Correction
> He says the approaches "should match but don't always". They reconcile **by construction**, not by coincidence — discrepancies are measurement error, not a conceptual mismatch.

> [!success] His good point — keep this
> High GDP ≠ good life for everybody. Oil states example: very high GDP, large parts of the population with little purchasing power. Distribution and living standards need **GINI**, **HDI**, **median income** — not GDP.

Useful pairing for the assignment: PwC's claim that AI could add **+14% to global GDP by 2030 ($15.7tn)** is a GDP claim and inherits exactly this weakness. → [[Key Figures from the Readings]]
